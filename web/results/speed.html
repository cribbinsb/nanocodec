Speed

Single-thread encode speed of every preset and reference on an embedded Arm core, two x86 cores (with and without AVX-512) and a server Arm core, the browser (WebAssembly) build, hardware encoders for scale, and how our decoders compare with the established ones.

Encode, one core

Encode CPU time per frame at 720p, one thread, at a fixed quantizer chosen for each encoder to land near the chart's middle rung on three development clips; real-time CBR costs our encoders slightly more. The Jetson core's clock is locked; on x86 (a rented AMD EPYC 9R45, Zen 5, and a rented AMD EPYC 7R13, Zen 3, which has no AVX-512, so every encoder runs its AVX2 code) and on server Arm (a rented AWS Graviton4, Neoverse V2) each run is paired with an x264 run in the same round, one pair at a time. Our times are then scaled per clip to an earlier session's x264 medium time, and the references keep the times of their own earlier sessions, so points from different sessions share one axis. The timing clips are not the clips the charts' quality is measured on.

In the browser (WebAssembly)

Frames per second of the browser build on one x86 core, single-threaded (the module has no threads), under V8 (node, cross-checked in headless Chromium). Decode: our CBR streams, decode alone. Encode: the demo's settings and its whole encode call (RGBA in). In brackets, the WebAssembly time over the native build's on the same streams. Fast only where the demo offers it.

Browser encode against ffmpeg.wasm

Encode speed of our browser module and of the official single-thread ffmpeg.wasm 0.12.10 (x264), on one x86 core in the same session, one 720p clip at one rate: the chart's middle-rung target, a peak of twice the target and a one-second buffer, the same on both sides; each encode's achieved rate is in the table. Both sides time their encode calls alone (ffmpeg.wasm's own per-call encode times; its module start-up is excluded). These are speed figures only: the browser encodes are single-rate encodes of one clip and were not scored, so no quality is shown for them. ffmpeg.wasm 0.12.10 is FFmpeg 5.1.4 built with --disable-asm: the compiler emits WASM SIMD128, but x264's and FFmpeg's hand-written SIMD kernels are off, so it is a weak yardstick for what a hand-vectorised port would reach.

Decoders against the established ones

Our decoders' time per frame, natively on one x86 core (AWS c8a.large, AMD EPYC 9R45) and on one Jetson Orin Nano core (Cortex-A78AE at a fixed clock), against FFmpeg's decoders (H.264, HEVC), libvpx (VP9) and VVdeC (VVC), each with FFmpeg's decoder beside it, dav1d through libavcodec (AV1), and AVM (AV2, a research reference decoder not built for speed) with dav2d (237229e, with its assembly) beside it; on x86 dav2d decodes both of our AV2 streams in less time than ours. Every reference is timed in process with the decode alone: packets already in memory, decoder open, probing and output excluded (our side includes building the decoder and scanning the container framing in memory). In the browser, against ffmpeg.wasm 0.12.10 (FFmpeg 5.1.4 built with --disable-asm: compiler-generated SIMD128 only; it has no AV1, VVC or AV2 decoder), timing the decode calls alone on both sides. Every decoder runs with one thread, though the references are normally run multi-threaded and are faster that way. Streams: ours, and third-party ones (x264, openh264, x265 with B-frames, IP cameras, SVT-AV1, libaom, vpxenc). Below 1 means ours took less time. Ours is release 0.6.0's PGO build (its decoder unchanged, plus only the in-process timing option); the caption under the chart says which machine, build and rounds.

Hardware encoders, for scale

NVIDIA NVENC on an RTX 5090 GPU in the same low-delay CBR on the development clips. Its speed is GPU wall-clock time with frames already in GPU memory, not CPU time, so it is not comparable with the tables above; the zero line here is the default-tuned x264 medium.
