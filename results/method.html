How it is measured

Everything on these pages is our own measurement, made with the project's evaluation harness (eval/nceval) and generated from the result files in the repository. This page states the method, the clip sets, the machines, the versions and the exact command lines.

The score

  • BD-rate (Bjøntegaard) on each encoder's achieved-rate curve against the zero line's, for the same quality: negative is fewer bits. Curves are interpolated (PCHIP) and only their reliable overlap counts; nothing is extrapolated.
  • J is PSNR-YUV, VMAF v1 and MS-SSIM in equal thirds, averaged over content classes. In charts rendered before 2026-11-23 (release 0.6.0's) it is also the mean of the full curve and its lower-rate half, so low rates count twice; charts rendered since use the whole overlap and show the BD by rate regime beside it. PSNR-YUV and VMAF v1 alone are shown too; MS-SSIM alone only where the run recorded it. PSNR-YUV weights Y:U:V 6:1:1. VMAF is libvmaf's model vmaf_v1.0.16_3d0h (the model file of Netflix/vmaf v3.2.1), scored on every frame at each clip's own size (no rescaling to 1080p) and averaged over frames. The model is calibrated for a 1080p display, so these values are relative and are not comparable with published 1080p-protocol VMAF. Points scored on our PC use VMAFx, a GPU (CUDA) build of libvmaf; points scored on rented machines use libvmaf 3.2.1 on the CPU. On 143 stored chart points (240p to 720p, 12 encoders) the two gave identical per-frame VMAF at libvmaf's six printed decimals and identical BD-VMAF on 9 encoder pairs; MS-SSIM agreed within 2.1e-5 per frame (validation). PSNR is computed per plane and per frame from exact squared-error sums, capped at 60 dB, averaged over frames.
  • No subjective test was run. x264 and x265 default to psychovisual tuning these metrics cannot judge, so the headline zero line is x264's own metric tuning (--tune zerolatency,psnr), and the default-tuned x264 is shown beside it. Our presets were tuned on this metric mix.
  • The reference encoders' points are always scored on a standard decoder's output. Ours scored on rented machines (the held-out and VC-1K charts and, since 2026-11-21, the chart refreshes) are scored on the first standard decoder's output, checked equal to the encoder's reconstruction frame by frame; ours scored on our PC are scored on the encoder's reconstruction, with PSNR from the encoder's own squared-error log (on 65 re-encoded points it matched libvmaf's PSNR within 5e-7 dB per plane per frame). Every build behind these numbers also passed the landing conformance check: short purpose-made streams of every shipped preset decoded bit-exactly by two standard decoders per format and by ours.

Clips and rates

    Clips are encoded only at rates at most half their source's bitrate, as many are already compressed. Our savings are largest at these low rates, and so are the references'; over a higher range (up to a few megabits at 720p) every gap shrinks, ours included, on the small subset with room for those rates (rate-control audit).

    Rate control and the buffer

    Each encoder's buffer use on every chart, measured at the target and at its own rate, and the buffer we keep: Rate control and the buffer.

    Settings

    • Low delay: every encoder in its own real-time rate control at the same targets, forward prediction only (no B-frames, no lookahead), one key frame, one thread, .
    • openh264 runs through our harness (tools/openh264-harness, a small C program over its API, as its own command-line tool cannot set every parameter): Constrained Baseline with CAVLC, medium complexity, camera real-time usage, adaptive quantization off, scene-change detection off, frame skipping off, one thread. With CABAC on it needed fewer bits on every clip of a five-clip probe; a CABAC, high-complexity openh264 row is not yet measured on these charts.
    • libvpx and libaom run real-time CBR without cyclic-refresh AQ (their default, --aq-mode=0); cyclic refresh, their usual real-time setting, needed fewer bits on average in a five-clip probe, with mixed signs by clip (audit).
    • x265 runs at its default AVX2 dispatch (AVX-512 off by its own default): --asm avx512 changes its stream on AVX-512 machines, so the same command would code different streams on the quality and timing machines.
    • Builds: ours is a PGO, LTO build for x86-64-v3; the references are their projects' stock release builds.
    • 8-bit 4:2:0 only. No 10-bit, no other chroma formats.
    • Ours do not code repeated frames that would not change the picture (holds); the references code every frame. Holds are rare on these clips.
    • The references are mature, general-purpose, multi-threaded encoders for many more uses; we measured only these settings, and their other modes may do better. Full rules (rate validity, chaining, flagged points): chart method.

    Machines

      Speed is measured at a fixed quantizer near the middle rung on three development clips, which a CBR encode costs our encoders slightly more than. The x86 machine is a rented AMD EPYC 9R45 (Zen 5; its SIMD extensions are listed under Machines), timing one encode pair at a time on a core of its own. Our times there are scaled per clip to an earlier session's x264 medium time (the raw times are kept in the timing file), and the references keep their own earlier sessions' times, so points from different sessions share one axis. The timing clips are not the clips the quality is measured on.

      Builds and versions

      Command lines

      Each encoder's real-time CBR command at the example rung (the harness writes these; tool paths shortened to program names), and the fixed-QP command its speed was timed with.

      Window length

      The headline windows are 150 frames with one key frame (5 s at 30 fps), and our presets were tuned on such windows. A short window favours an encoder that invests in its first key frame, as ours do. The same build was therefore also measured over 600 frames with a key frame every 10 s, on held-out sources long enough for it. In brackets, 95% bootstrap intervals.
